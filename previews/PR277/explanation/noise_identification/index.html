Noise identification

The confidence interval of a deviation depends on which power-law noise dominates at that averaging time, and so do the bias corrections of the total and Thêo estimators (SP1065 §5.5.1). Unless the noise type is stated, every estimator finds it from the record with identify_noise, one averaging time at a time. This page describes the tests, where the package departs from the published procedure, and how far a label can be trusted.

What depends on the label

The label sets the equivalent degrees of freedom (EDF) of the estimate. Here is one Allan deviation, at 16 averaged points of a 1024-point record, under each label in turn:

using SigmaTau, Random

p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 1024, 1.0; sigma1 = Dict(:WHFM => 1e-12), rng = Xoshiro(1))
for n in (:WHPM, :FLPM, :WHFM, :FLFM, :RWFM)
    r = adev(p, [64]; noise = n)
    lo, hi = round.((ci_lower(r)[1], ci_upper(r)[1]) ./ r.dev[1]; digits = 2)
    println(rpad(n, 6), "EDF ", lpad(round(r.edf[1]; digits = 1), 5),
            "   interval ", lo, " to ", hi, " of the estimate")
end
WHPM  EDF 478.4   interval 0.97 to 1.03 of the estimate
FLPM  EDF  78.1   interval 0.93 to 1.09 of the estimate
WHFM  EDF  21.8   interval 0.88 to 1.19 of the estimate
FLFM  EDF  17.0   interval 0.86 to 1.23 of the estimate
RWFM  EDF  13.3   interval 0.85 to 1.27 of the estimate

Taking one FM noise for another moves the interval a little. Taking FM for PM makes it several times too narrow: the overlapping terms of the estimate are nearly uncorrelated under white PM, so its EDF follows the record length and not the number of averaged points. The rules below are weighted against that mistake.

The lag-1 autocorrelation test

The main test is that of Riley and Greenhall (2004), also given in SP1065 §5.5.5 and §5.5.6. For averaging factor $m$:

  1. The phase is decimated by $m$, which leaves the phase record at sample interval $mτ_0$, and a least-squares quadratic is removed from it, as allantools does.
  2. The lag-1 autocorrelation $r_1$ of the series gives $δ = r_1/(1 + r_1)$.
  3. If $δ ≥ 0.25$ the series is differenced and step 2 repeated, up to dmax times: twice for the Allan family, three times for the Hadamard family.
  4. After $d$ differences the exponent of the noise is $α = 2 - 2(δ + d)$.

The estimate is a real number. On the record as sampled ($m = 1$) it is rounded to the nearest integer, as the paper does. The test is reliable from about 30 points (SP1065 §5.5.6), and it is used while $N/m ≥ 30$.

Samples farther than five standard deviations from the mean of the record are left out of the test.

Decimated records

The test is derived for discrete-time noise with the spectrum $(2 \sin πf)^{-2δ}$. A record decimated by $m ≥ 2$ behaves as a continuous process read at intervals, and on three noise types the difference is large enough to change the label. The package allows for each.

Flicker FM and random-walk FM. The second differences of decimated phase have lag-1 correlations of $(9 \ln 3 - 16 \ln 2)/(8 \ln 2) ≈ -0.217$ and 1/4 on these two, where the discrete-time model has −1/3 and 0. The estimate therefore settles on $α = -1.45$ and −2.4, not −1 and −2. After a third difference it reads −3.0, −3.93 and −4.57 on random-walk, flicker-walk and random-run FM. All five values follow from the generalized autocovariances of Greenhall and Riley (2003). For $m ≥ 2$ the limits between the FM labels are placed halfway between the readings, at −0.72, −1.92, −3.46 and −4.25, where rounding puts them at the half-integers. Rounding at −1.5 cuts through the middle of the flicker FM readings: in simulation (4096 points) flicker FM keeps its label in 71 % of records at $m = 8$ and 48 % at $m = 64$ with rounding, and in 100 % and 87 % with these limits.

Flicker PM. Decimation whitens it. On records of 16 384 points the estimate averages 1.0 at $m = 1$, 1.5 at $m = 16$ and 1.8 at $m = 256$, and the test alone calls flicker PM white in 45 % of records at $m = 16$ and in 94 % at $m = 256$. From $m = 4$ a white PM result is therefore checked with $R(n)$, the ratio of the modified to the plain Allan variance (SP1065 §5.5.2 and §5.10), which reads every sample of the record. $R(n)$ is $1/m$ on white PM and falls only as $1/\ln m$ on flicker PM. A result nearer the flicker PM value is relabeled. Flicker PM then keeps its label in 99 % of those records or more, and white PM loses its own in 0.3 % at most.

PM or FM. With few decimated points the test takes FM for PM, the costly mistake: in simulation (8192 points) white FM in 8 % of records at 64 points and in 22 % at 32. $R(n)$ is 1/2, 27/40 and 33/40 on white, flicker and random-walk FM, against 0.26 on flicker PM at $m = 16$ and less beyond. From $m = 16$ a PM result stands only if $R(n)$ lies nearer its flicker PM value than 1/2. One that fails is reported as white FM. With this rule, and with the conditions of the next section below 30 points, none of the 1500 simulated records of white FM was labeled PM at any averaging factor from 64 to 2048.

Fewer than 30 averaged points

Below 30 points the lag-1 estimate is too noisy, and the package turns to the $B_1$ ratio, as Stable32 does. $B_1$ is the standard variance of the $m$-averaged frequency values divided by their Allan variance. Its expected value depends on the number of values $N$ and on the slope $μ$ of the Allan variance, $σ_y^2(τ) ∝ τ^μ$ (SP1065 §5.5.2 and §5.7):

\[B_1(N, μ) = \frac{N\,(1 - N^{μ})}{2\,(N - 1)\,(1 - 2^{μ})} ,\]

which is $N/2$ on random-walk FM, $N \ln N / (2 (N-1) \ln 2)$ on flicker FM, 1 on white FM and $2(N+1)/(3N)$ on PM. The measured ratio is compared with the geometric means of neighboring values, and a PM result is split into white and flicker PM with $R(n)$. The test stops at random-walk FM.

Three choices here are the package's own.

  • The length $N$. The expected ratios are evaluated at the number of averaged values the ratio was computed from, which is the $N$ of the definition. identify_noise(...; b1_length=:record) evaluates them at the length of the whole record, which follows Stable32's labels more closely. At that length the limit for random-walk FM lies far above what a few values can reach. In simulation (1024 points, 6 to 24 averaged values) the record length finds random-walk FM in at most 2 % of records and flicker FM in 23 to 46 %, where the default finds them in 59 to 74 % and 34 to 78 %. White FM pays for it: 71 to 96 % against 86 to 100 %.
  • A floor of 6 values. With 2 to 4 values the ratio is close to a coin toss, so below 6 averaged values the result is the one found at $⌊N/6⌋$, the longest averaging factor that still has 6. The value 6 comes from simulation, not from the literature.
  • Two conditions for PM. In a sum of power-law noises the dominant type only gets redder toward longer $τ$. A result from fewer than 30 points is therefore PM only if the result at $⌊N/30⌋$, the longest averaging factor that has 30 points, is PM, and $R(n)$ at $m$ agrees. With 6 points or more $R(n)$ must lie below the midpoint of $1/m$ and 1/2, the value it takes where white PM and white FM hold equal shares of the Allan variance, and it then decides between white and flicker PM. With fewer it must lie on the white PM side of the limit between the two PM types, and it is read at $⌊N/4⌋$ where $m$ is larger. Below $m = 16$, which a record shorter than 480 samples reaches with fewer than 30 points, a PM result of the $B_1$ test takes the place of $R(n)$. Every other result is the FM type of the $B_1$ ratio, with white FM for a PM result of it.

The conditions guard both ways. FM noise is not labeled PM on the strength of a few points, and white PM is not labeled FM: in simulation (1024 points) the $B_1$ ratio alone takes white PM for FM in one record in six at 16 averaged points and in one in three at 8, which puts the FM bias factor of the total estimators on PM noise, and with the conditions white PM keeps its label down to 2 points. The cost falls on flicker PM with fewer than 6 averaged points, which was labeled white FM in four records in five or more (78 % of the records of 1024 points, 86 % of those of

  1. and flicker PM in none. Its interval is then wide, and the total,

modified total and Hadamard total variances read 19, 10 and 25 % high at 4 points.

With b1_length=:record neither the floor nor the conditions apply: the result of the $B_1$ test stands, subject to the $R(n)$ check of the previous section. identify_noise(...; b1=false) leaves the ratio out, so that an FM result from fewer than 30 points is the type found at $⌊N/30⌋$.

The lag-1 test removes a quadratic from each decimated series, but the standard variance of the $B_1$ ratio is taken from the record as given. A linear frequency drift that dominates the Allan deviation at the longest averaging times is therefore labeled random-walk FM there by the Allan family. The deviation itself rises with the drift, so remove the drift first with detrend if those points matter.

The Hadamard family

The Hadamard estimators converge for two more noise types, flicker-walk FM ($α = -3$) and random-run FM ($α = -4$), so their labels are found with up to three differences (SP1065 §5.5.6). They are also blind to linear frequency drift, and by default their labels are too: the noise type is identified on the record less its least-squares quadratic.

On that record the $B_1$ ratio is not used. Its limits are those of a record with the drift left in: in simulation, at 16 averaged values, removing the quadratic lowered the mean ratio from 7.8 to 3.2 on random-walk FM and from 2.2 to 1.7 on flicker FM. The test also stops at random-walk FM, two types short of what the family converges for. With fewer than 30 averaged points an FM label is therefore the one found at $⌊N/30⌋$, the longest averaging factor that has 30, and a PM label is subject to the two conditions above. A change from one FM type to another beyond that averaging time is not seen. State it with noise.

remove_drift=false on hdev, mhdev, htdev and htotdev identifies the noise on the record as given, with the $B_1$ ratio, which is what Stable32 does. The deviation is the same either way. In simulation, on white FM with a drift that dominates the Allan deviation at the longest averaging times, the default labeled the points with 16, 8 and 4 averaged values white FM in 98 % of records, and remove_drift=false labeled them random-walk FM in every record. On flicker-walk and random-run FM it is the other way round: with remove_drift=false neither was found in any record below 30 points, where the default kept the labels it found at 30 (tables below).

From 64 points up the label is right in at least 86 % of records. Flicker FM is the hard case throughout: at 32 points the lag-1 test finds it in half of the records. Below 30 points the Hadamard family repeats the FM label found at 30, an FM label of the Allan family is weak evidence of the type, and flicker PM is not found with fewer than 6 points.

The intervals hold up better than the labels, because the wrong label is usually a neighbor. In the same simulation the 68.3 % interval of the Allan, modified Allan, Hadamard and modified Hadamard deviations, with the label found automatically, holds the true value in 64 to 76 % of records on every FM noise at every $N/m$ in the tables. The low end is the Allan deviation on random-walk FM at 8 and 4 points. On PM noise the figure is 66 to 73 % at 32 points or more and 66 to 78 % below, with one exception: at 4 points the intervals of the Allan and Hadamard deviations of flicker PM, which is labeled white FM there, hold the true value in 96 % of records. They are on the safe side and wide: the upper limit is 2.2 and 2.6 times the lower, where the true label gives 1.25 and 1.3.

A record whose noise type changes is harder, because near the crossing no label is right. On simulated records where white or flicker PM gives way to FM between 1/64 and 1/8 of the record, the 68.3 % interval of the Allan, Hadamard and total deviations held the true value in 64 to 96 % of records at 16 to 4 averaged points, and that of the modified total and Hadamard total deviations in 58 to 86 %.

Three things a label is not:

  • It is not a component of the record. Where two noise types cross, the test reads the type between them, as the example below shows.
  • It is not evidence of noise. A periodic or wandering term moves the labels near its time scale: in simulation a sinusoid on white FM was read as flicker FM and random-walk FM at averaging times a sixteenth and an eighth of its period.
  • It is not shared between the families. The Allan and Hadamard families identify the noise separately and can differ at the same $τ$.
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 16384, 1.0; sigma1 = Dict(:WHFM => 1e-12, :RWFM => 1e-14),
              rng = Xoshiro(1))
r = adev(p)
foreach((τ, n) -> println(lpad(Int(τ), 5), " s  ", n), r.tau, r.noise_type)

Stating the noise type

When the noise type is known, state it. Every estimator that reports an interval takes noise:

r = adev(p, [64, 128, 256]; noise = :WHFM)
println(join(r.noise_type, " "))
WHFM WHFM WHFM
noiseLabel at each $τ$
:autoidentified from the record (the default)
a label, such as :WHFMthat type everywhere
a vector of labelsone per averaging factor
a PowerLawFitthe strongest component of the fitted model at that $τ$

A fitted model gives labels that change once, at the crossing, with no flicker FM between:

f = powerlaw_fit(p; alphas = [0, -2])
println(join(adev(p; noise = f).noise_type, " "))
WHFM WHFM WHFM WHFM WHFM WHFM WHFM WHFM RWFM RWFM RWFM RWFM RWFM

The command line takes --noise auto, --noise fit or a label, and the explorer has the same choice under Noise type. A type redder than an estimator converges for counts as the reddest it does: :RRFM given to adev is taken as :RWFM.

Coming from Stable32 lists the points at which these labels differ from Stable32's, with the option that reproduces each where there is one.
