Holdover

A clock that loses its reference runs on what it learned while it had one. holdover measures how well that works on a phase record. It learns the frequency from a training stretch, and the drift as well with drift = true, lets the clock run from the last reading of the stretch, at time $t$, and compares with what the record did next:

\[e(τ) = x(t + τ) - x(t) - \hat{y}\,τ - \tfrac{1}{2} \hat{D}\,τ^2 .\]

That is the time interval error $x(t + τ) - x(t)$ of ITU-T G.810, clause 4.5.14, less the learned frequency and drift. Every sample that the record allows is a start, as in the sliding test of Delporte et al. (2000), so the statistics at each holdover time come from the whole record.

using SigmaTau, Random

# white FM of 1e-11 at 1 s that gives way to random-walk FM near 400 s
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 65_536, 1.0; h = Dict(0 => 2e-22, -2 => 1e-28),
              rng = Xoshiro(1))
h = holdover(p; train = 512, tau = [16, 64, 256, 1024], limit = 1e-9)

The columns are the holdover time, the RMS error, the error that 95 % of the starts stay within, the largest error and the number of starts. With limit, the line above the table gives the holdover time at which the 95 % column reaches that error.

The training stretch

No clock model enters the test. The learned frequency is the slope of a least-squares line through the stretch, and what limits it depends on the noise. For white FM of level $q_1$ and random-walk FM of level $q_2$ (Noise levels) and a line over $T$ seconds, the variance of the error is

\[σ_e^2(τ) = q_1 τ \left(1 + \frac{6τ}{5T}\right) + q_2 \left(\frac{τ^3}{3} + \frac{13\,T τ^2}{35}\right) .\]

In each bracket the first term is the noise of the clock over $τ$ and the second the error of the learned frequency. The first terms, $q_1 τ$ and $q_2 τ^3/3$, are $τ^2 σ_y^2(τ)$, the mean square of the optimum prediction on each noise (Allan (1987), Table III). The second are the price of learning the frequency from a finite stretch: a longer one averages the white FM down and lets the frequency walk away, so there is a best train, $\sqrt{42 q_1 / 13 q_2}$, about 400 s for this clock.

q1, q2 = 1e-22, 2π^2 * 1e-28              # h₀/2 and 2π²h₋₂
σe(τ, T) = sqrt(q1 * τ * (1 + 6τ / 5T) + q2 * (τ^3 / 3 + 13T * τ^2 / 35))
T = [64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 4096]
# the stretch, the RMS error measured 256 s on, and the form
[T  [holdover(p; train, tau = [256]).rms[1] for train in T]  σe.(256, T)]

The form is that of a line through a continuous stretch. With 64 samples in the stretch it holds within 0.1 % on white FM and 2 % on random-walk FM, and with 1024 within 0.2 % on both. One record scatters about it: over 200 records like this one the measured RMS was within 1 % of the form in the median at every stretch, and four records in five were within 3 % of it with 64 samples in the stretch and within 17 % with 4096.

A parabola, drift = true, also learns a frequency drift. Under a line a drift $D$ leaves a mean error of $\tfrac{1}{2} D τ (τ + T)$, which shows in the mean of the result. The parabola pays for its third coefficient in noise. Its RMS error over that of a line, on a clock without drift and with 256 samples in the stretch:

With anchor = false the value of the fit at $t$ takes the place of the last reading, which is the extrapolated fit that Delporte et al. analyze. It averages the white PM of the readings down, and on a clock with random-walk FM it starts from the misfit of the fit.

Predicted from a noise model

The same call on a noise model gives the error that a record of that noise should show. The model is a PowerLawFit, a NoiseFit, a ClockModel, or a Dict of the levels $h_α$, such as those of a data sheet.

fit = powerlaw_fit(p)
pred = holdover(fit; train = 512, tau = [16, 64, 256, 1024], limit = 1e-9)

The error is a weighted sum of phase samples, so its variance follows without approximation from the spectrum of the sampled record, the one that powerlaw_sigma integrates. The flicker noises are predicted on the same footing as the others, and no state model stands in for them. The tables of this page are such predictions. For a long stretch and anchor = false the prediction gives the closed forms of Delporte et al. (2000), Eqs. (11)–(13) and (17)–(19) for a parabola and for a line on white, flicker and random-walk FM, within 0.2 % at 4096 samples in the stretch.

The prediction holds noise only. A drift that a line does not learn adds its mean error.

With a Kalman filter as the predictor

A record and a ClockModel together run the test with the Kalman filter in place of the fit. The filter passes once over the record, and from every sample past the first train seconds its estimate is carried $τ$ ahead with no further readings and compared with the reading there.

m = clock_model(fit)
k = holdover(p, m; tau = [16, 64, 256, 1024])
HoldoverResult(4 pts, τ∈[16, 1024] s, trained on 1.638e+04 s)
         tau         rms         p95       worst         n     claimed
          16   4.142e-11   8.107e-11   1.711e-10     49137   4.097e-11
          64   9.333e-11   1.853e-10   3.752e-10     49089   9.013e-11
         256   2.688e-10   5.174e-10   9.681e-10     48897    2.49e-10
        1024    1.17e-09   2.267e-09    3.79e-09     48129   1.077e-09

claimed is the standard deviation that the filter itself gives the error: holdover_error with the noise of the reading it is compared with. The two columns side by side test the model where it matters for holdover. An rms well above claimed says that the model holds too little noise at that $τ$.

A model made from a fit keeps the flicker FM that the fit finds, as Markov states where it holds a quarter of the model's Allan variance (Where the states sit). This clock has none and the fit finds a little, too little for a state, so m has two states. With flicker = false that noise is left out of the model, and in simulation the filter of a clock on its flicker floor claimed 1.4 to 8.7 times less than the error it made (What the states buy). sigmatau holdover --kalman and the explorer fit the model to the first training stretch alone, so that the levels are not learned from the readings that the predictions are compared with.

With the right model the filter's prediction is the conditional mean of the phase, which no predictor betters in mean square (Särkkä and Svensson (2023), §2.4 and Theorem 6.6). A least-squares line comes close at its best stretch and falls behind away from it. For a clock of white and random-walk FM, the standard deviation of the line's error over that of the filter's, at holdover times from a tenth of the best stretch to six times it:

The measured RMS errors of a simulated record of that clock gave the same ratios within 5 %.

At the longest holdover times

The ratio of rms to claimed is least certain at the longest holdover times. The RMS there rests on few independent trials, and the claim on what one stretch shows of the slow noise. In simulation, on records of 8192 samples with the first quarter as the stretch, the ratio at 2048 s was, in four records out of five:

ClockWith the true modelWith the model fitted to the stretch
white FM0.56 to 1.560.33 to 1.29
white FM, random-walk FM from 390 s0.55 to 1.370.60 to 8.6

At $τ_0$ it was 0.98 to 1.04 with the fitted model. On white FM the fit found some flicker or random-walk FM in 61 of the 100 records, and in the median the claim was twice that of the true model. On the second clock the fit found less than 1 % of the random-walk FM in half of the records, and in the median the claim was half that of the true model. A ratio well above 1 at long $τ$ says that the stretch was too short to show the slow noise.

The fit of the stretch has points up to between an eighth and a quarter of it, 256 s here, and a claim past that extrapolates the model. On the same records the claim of the fitted model over that of the true model was 0.96 to 1.75 at 256 s and 0.96 to 6.0 at 2048 s on white FM, and 0.62 to 1.30 and 0.20 to 1.63 on the second clock. sigmatau holdover --kalman gives that averaging time as fit_tau_max, and the explorer dots the claimed curve past it.

Holdover and the time deviation

The time variance is the mean square of an error of the same kind. With $\bar{x}$ the phase averaged over $τ$, the definitions of $\mathrm{Mod}\,σ_y(τ)$ and $σ_x(τ)$ (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eqs. (A.26) and (A.29)) give

\[σ_x^2(τ) = \tfrac{1}{6} \left\langle \left( \bar{x}(t + 2τ) - 2\,\bar{x}(t + τ) + \bar{x}(t) \right)^2 \right\rangle ,\]

and the bracket is the next $τ$-average less the line through the two before it. So $\sqrt{6}$ tdev is the RMS error of a holdover test that learns the frequency from two $τ$-averages and predicts the third. The third difference does the same with three averages and a parabola, drift learned: its mean square is $6τ^2\,\mathrm{Mod}\,σ_H^2(τ)$, which makes that error $\sqrt{20}$ htdev. The Hadamard time deviation has the steps.

grow = k in place of train gives the measured test the matching stretch, $k$ times each holdover time, so that a longer holdover is learned over a longer stretch:

g = holdover(p; grow = 2)                 # a line over 2τ, then τ ahead
mτ = Int.(g.tau ./ p.tau0)
[g.tau  g.rms ./ (sqrt(6) .* tdev(p, mτ; ci = false).dev)]

The two differ in the readings: TDEV averages them over $τ$, and the test compares one reading with a least-squares line. At $τ = τ_0$ an average is a reading and the two are equal. Above it the ratio depends on the noise type. On FM noise it settles within a few octaves, and on PM noise it keeps rising. At long $τ$:

On FM noise the test runs parallel to the scaled time deviation, 1.2 to 1.4 times it, so a scaled time deviation read as a holdover error understates the error of a least-squares fit by that factor. On white PM the averages smooth what a single reading does not, and the two part. With a fixed train there is no such relation, because the stretch no longer scales with $τ$.

SigmaTau.holdover — Function
holdover(data::PhaseData; train, grow, tau, drift=false, anchor=true,
         limit=nothing, confidence=0.95) → HoldoverResult

The time error of a clock that runs on without its reference, measured on the record itself at every start the record allows.

A line is fitted to train seconds of phase, or a parabola with drift = true. From the last reading of the stretch, at time $t$, the clock is left to run on the fitted frequency $\hat{y}$ and drift $\hat{D}$:

\[e(τ) = x(t + τ) - x(t) - \hat{y}\,τ - \tfrac{1}{2} \hat{D}\,τ^2 ,\]

the time interval error of ITU-T G.810, clause 4.5.14 less what the stretch taught. The stretch then moves on by one sample, and so on through the record, as in the test of Delporte et al. (2000). The result holds the RMS, the confidence quantile, the largest value and the mean of $e$ at each holdover time.

With anchor = false the value of the fit at $t$ takes the place of the reading $x(t)$. That is the extrapolated fit of that paper: it averages white PM down, and on a clock with random-walk FM it starts from the misfit of the fit.

With grow = k in place of train, the stretch is $k$ times each holdover time, so that a longer holdover is learned over a longer stretch. At grow = 2, or 3 with drift, the test is the counterpart of the time deviations: $\sqrt{6}$ tdev is the RMS error of extrapolating the next τ-average of the phase from the two before it, and $\sqrt{20}$ htdev that of the next from the three before it. Holdover and the time deviation compares the three.

The error includes the white PM of the readings. NaN samples are gaps: a start is skipped when gaps take more than half of its training stretch, the reading it would be compared with or, when anchored, its last reading.

Arguments

  • data: the phase record of the clock against its reference.

Keywords

  • train: length of the training stretch in seconds, a whole multiple of τ₀; a quarter of the record by default.
  • grow: the stretch as a multiple of each holdover time, in place of train; 2 or more, and 3 or more with drift.
  • tau: holdover times in seconds, rising whole multiples of τ₀. By default the octave multiples of τ₀ up to train, or to half of what the record has left after the stretch where that is less, and with grow those that leave half the record as trials.
  • drift: fit a parabola, which learns a frequency drift. Under a line, a drift $D$ leaves a mean error that grows as $\tfrac{1}{2} D τ (τ + T)$ for a stretch of $T$ seconds.
  • anchor: start each trial from the last reading of the stretch, or from the value of the fit there.
  • limit: a time error in seconds. time_to_limit is then the holdover time at which the quantile reaches it: 0 when the first tau is past it already, Inf when the last has not reached it.
  • confidence: level of the quantile.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 65536, 1.0; h = Dict(0 => 2e-22, -2 => 1e-30),
              rng = Xoshiro(1))
h = holdover(p; train = 4096, limit = 1e-9)
h.rms                 # at h.tau
h.time_to_limit       # seconds within 1 ns, in 95 % of the trials
source
holdover(data::PhaseData, model::ClockModel; train, tau, limit=nothing,
         confidence=0.95) → HoldoverResult

The same test with a Kalman filter as the predictor.

The filter for model runs once over the record (kalman_filter). At every sample past the first train seconds its estimate is carried τ ahead without further readings and compared with the reading there. claimed is the standard deviation that the filter itself gives that error,

\[σ^2(τ) = \bigl[Φ(τ)\,P\,Φ(τ)^\mathsf{T} + Q(τ)\bigr]_{11} + R ,\]

which is holdover_error with the noise of the reading it is compared with, averaged over the trials. A model with flicker PM reads its Markov phase states along with the phase.

rms agrees with claimed when the model fits the clock, and an rms above it says that the noise of the model is too low. A model made from a noise fit keeps the flicker FM that the fit finds, as clock_model does by default. With flicker = false that noise is left out of the model, and in simulation the filter of a clock on its flicker floor claimed several times less than the error it made (Flicker states).

With the right model the prediction is the conditional mean of the phase, which no other predictor betters in mean square (Särkkä and Svensson (2023), §2.4 and Theorem 6.6). A least-squares fit therefore holds no better at any training stretch; at its best stretch it comes within a few percent (With a Kalman filter as the predictor).

Arguments

  • data: the phase record of the clock against its reference.
  • model: the ClockModel of the clock, with the tau0 of data.

Keywords

  • train: seconds from the start of the record that the filter is given before the first trial, a whole multiple of τ₀; a quarter of the record by default.
  • tau: holdover times in seconds, rising whole multiples of τ₀; by default the octave multiples of τ₀ up to half of what the record has past train.
  • limit, confidence: as for the least-squares test.

A trial is left out when the reading it starts from or the reading it is compared with is missing: a start inside a gap would predict from an estimate that has already run free.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, q1 = 1e-22, q2 = 1e-26)
p, _ = simulate_clock(m, 65536; rng = Xoshiro(1))
h = holdover(p, m; tau = [1, 10, 100, 1000])
h.rms ./ h.claimed    # near 1
source
holdover(model; train, grow, tau, tau0, drift=false, anchor=true,
         limit=nothing, confidence=0.95) → HoldoverPrediction

The same test predicted from a noise model: the standard deviation of the error that holdover measures on a record, for a clock with the power-law noise of model.

The error is a weighted sum of phase samples, so its variance follows without approximation from the spectrum of the sampled record, $h_α\,[\sin(πfτ_0)/(πτ_0)]^α$ up to $1/(2τ_0)$, flicker noise included. budget holds the share of each noise type. For the extrapolated fit, anchor = false, a long stretch gives the closed forms of Delporte et al. (2000), Eqs. (11)–(13) and (17)–(19). The quantile is the Gaussian one, 1.96 σ at 95 %.

A line takes $α$ from 2 to −2, and a parabola −3 and −4 as well. A frequency drift $D$ that the model does not hold adds the mean $\tfrac{1}{2} D τ (τ + T)$ under a line over $T$ seconds, and nothing under a parabola.

Arguments

  • model: a PowerLawFit, a NoiseFit, a ClockModel, or a Dict of the coefficients $h_α$ keyed by $α$ or by noise type, as for powerlaw_sigma.

Keywords

  • train: length of the training stretch in seconds, a whole multiple of tau0. It has no default here.
  • grow: the stretch as a multiple of each holdover time, in place of train; tau must then be given.
  • tau: holdover times in seconds, rising whole multiples of tau0; by default the octave multiples of tau0 up to train.
  • tau0: sample interval in seconds; that of a fit or a clock model by default, and needed with a Dict.
  • drift, anchor, limit, confidence: as for the test on a record.

Examples

julia> white = Dict(:WHFM => 2e-22);

julia> h = holdover(white; tau0 = 1.0, train = 3600, tau = [900, 3600]);

HoldoverResult

Output of holdover on a record: the statistics of the time error at each holdover time, over every start the record allows.

  • time_to_limit::Float64: Holdover time (s) at which quantile reaches limit; NaN when none.
