Related software

Other programs compute the statistics of this package. This page describes the ones it has been compared with, or that cover the same ground, in the versions current in November 2026. The descriptions follow each program's own documentation, and the comparisons state what was compared and how closely the results agree.

Stable32

Stable32 is the Windows program of Hamilton Technical Services, whose W. J. Riley wrote the NIST handbook that this package follows (Riley, 2008). It was sold from 1982 until 2017 and then given to the IEEE UFFC Society for free distribution. The current version is 1.62, of 2016 (www.wriley.com).

Its manual (Hamilton Technical Services, 2008) lists the Allan and Hadamard deviations in their normal and overlapping forms, the modified Allan and time deviations, the total, modified total, time total and Hadamard total deviations, Thêo1, ThêoBR and ThêoH, MTIE and TIE rms. Each run identifies the noise type and reports χ² confidence limits. The program also plots and edits data, removes drift and outliers, computes spectra and dynamic stability, and separates three clocks with the three-cornered hat. It has functions this package lacks: dead-time corrections, frequency jump detection, filtering and a histogram.

The cross-check suite compares this package with Stable32's result files on its own test record, the 1000-point test suite of SP1065 §12.4, and on a flicker PM record from its noise generator, with the options that select Stable32's conventions. The deviations agree within $5 × 10^{-5}$, one unit of the last of the five digits Stable32 prints. On the test record the noise types are the same at every averaging time compared, and the confidence limits agree within 1 part in 1000, apart from the cases listed in the cross-check report.

This package departs from Stable32 where the literature or a simulation supports a different choice, in the noise identification, the bias factors and the handling of drift. Coming from Stable32 lists each difference and the option that reproduces Stable32.

allantools

allantools is a Python package by Anders E. E. Wallin and contributors under the LGPL, version 3 or later (Wallin et al., 2024). Version 2024.6 has the functions adev, oadev, mdev, tdev, hdev, ohdev, pdev, totdev, mtotdev, ttotdev, htotdev, theo1, mtie and tierms, the gap-tolerant gradev and the Groslambert codeviation gcodev. Each returns the deviation with the number of terms and an error estimate of the deviation over the square root of that number. The equivalent degrees of freedom, the χ² interval and the lag-1 noise identification are separate functions (edf_greenhall, confidence_interval, autocorr_noise_id). The total deviations are returned without bias correction. The package also has real-time estimators, noise generators and classes for datasets and plots.

The cross-check suite runs allantools 2024.6 on seven power-law records of 4096 points and on two reference records, and compares every function above except gcodev over a grid of averaging factors. The deviations agree within $10^{-9}$ ($10^{-8}$ for the modified Allan and time deviations), and so do the EDF of edf_greenhall and the χ² factors. The lag-1 estimates of the noise exponent agree within $10^{-6}$. The two packages differ in the EDF they give the total deviations; the cross-check report lists the cases. A continuous-integration job installs allantools and AllanDeviations.jl and repeats the comparison on every pull request.

Coming from allantools maps its calls onto this package.

AllanDeviations.jl

AllanDeviations.jl is a Julia package by Julien Kluge under the MIT license (Kluge, 2022). Version 0.3.0 computes the Allan, modified Allan, Hadamard, time and total deviations and MTIE (allandev, mallandev, hadamarddev, timedev, totaldev, mtie), the Allan and Hadamard deviations with or without overlap. Each returns the deviation, the number of terms and an error estimate of the deviation over the square root of that number.

The cross-check suite compares all six on the seven power-law records. The deviations agree within $10^{-9}$ ($10^{-8}$ for the modified Allan and time deviations), and MTIE is identical.

SigmaTheta

SigmaTheta is a set of command-line programs in C by François Vernotte, Université de Franche-Comté, under the CeCILL license (Vernotte, 2025). Its programs compute the Allan, modified Allan, Hadamard and parabolic deviations, give the Allan and modified Allan deviations 95 % and 68 % confidence intervals, and find the dominant noise type at each $τ$ from asymptotes fitted to the deviation curve. Others remove linear drift and simulate noise records. For three clocks they compute the Groslambert codeviation and its confidence interval by the KLTG and KLTS methods (Vernotte and Lantz, 2019; Lantz et al., 2019).

groslambert computes the KLTS interval with interval=:klts, and SigmaTheta is its authors' implementation. On three simulated clocks of 4096 points the Groslambert codeviations of the two programs agreed to every printed digit, and given the same inputs the KLTS bounds agreed within 2 %. The comparison was made once, against the version of April 2025, and is not part of the test suite. The suite does hold pdev to nine values on the 1000-point test suite that the allantools tests give, computed there with the PDev program of SigmaTheta 3.0 (October 2023). They agree within $10^{-11}$.

ntpstats

ntpstats is a Python toolkit for validating and studying network time synchronization by Thiago de Freitas, under the MIT license (de Freitas, 2026). Beside its tools for NTP and PTP, version 3.9.0 has the Allan, modified Allan, time and Hadamard deviations, the total, modified total, time total and Hadamard total deviations, Thêo1, ThêoBR and ThêoH, MTIE and TIE rms, with χ² intervals, lag-1 noise identification, a power-law fit, spectra, an N-cornered hat and masks. Its documentation reports agreement with Stable32 1.62.

This package has not been compared with ntpstats.

In the last row Stable32 has the classical three-cornered hat, and the other programs have the Groslambert covariance as well.

The modified Hadamard deviation is defined by Bregni and Jmoda (2006) and described in SP1065 §5.2.10. The modified Hadamard total deviation is constructed in this package, after an outline by Vernotte and Howe (2000) (MHTOTDEV bias and EDF). None of the programs above computes either, so their tests rest on identities, on direct evaluation of the defining sums and, for the total, on the simulations described on that page.
