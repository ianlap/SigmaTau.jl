Kalman filter

kalman_filter estimates the phase and the frequency of one clock from its phase record, and the frequency drift where the model has a drift state. It runs on a ClockModel, whose states and noise levels The clock model derives. The same filter is the predictor of the holdover test and the estimator of the steering loop, and the Kalman time scales run one over several clocks.

From a record to a filter

A fit of the noise gives the model, and the model gives the filter.

using SigmaTau, Random

# white FM of 1e-12 at 1 s on a flicker floor of 1e-13, read with 1 ps of white PM
h = Dict(2 => 8π^2 * 1e-24, 0 => 2e-24, -1 => 1e-26 / 2log(2))
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 16_384, 1.0; h, rng = Xoshiro(1))
fit = powerlaw_fit(p)
PowerLawFit to mhdev (12 points)
  h2   WHPM  8.129e-23
  h1   FLPM  0
  h0   WHFM  2.053e-24
  h-1  FLFM  7.08e-27
  h-2  RWFM  0
m = clock_model(fit)
5-state ClockModel, τ₀ = 1.0 s
  R  1.03e-24 s²
  q1 1.027e-24 s
  q2 0 1/s
  flicker FM 7.08e-27, 3 Markov states, 42.17 to 4217 s

The model keeps the flicker FM that the fit found, as Markov states over the averaging times at which it matters (Flicker states).

kf = kalman_filter(p, m)
kf.x[end, 1:2]                  # phase (s) and frequency at the last sample
2-element Vector{Float64}:
 -6.252125607340409e-13
 -1.3277122652216667e-13

Each row of kf.x is the state at one sample, and kf.P[:, :, k] is its covariance at sample $k$. A NaN sample is a gap, which the filter predicts across without an update. The filter needs no starting values: it takes them from a line through the first readings.

kalman_smooth runs back over the result, so that every estimate uses the readings after it as well as those before. That suits a record analyzed afterwards. The filter alone gives what a clock running in real time can know.

ks = kalman_smooth(kf)
# 1σ of the phase at sample 8000, smoothed over filtered
sqrt(ks.P[1, 1, 8000] / kf.P[1, 1, 8000])
0.8470022702845915

Testing the model

The record tests the model. Under the right model the innovations, each reading less its prediction, are uncorrelated and have the variance that the filter expects, and innovation_check tests both.

innovation_check(kf)
(nis = 1.0115323508710081, nis_bounds = (0.978461102904273, 1.0217701335769125), acf = 0.009147921439760724, acf_bound = 0.0153122186292192, ok = true)

nis is the mean square of the innovations over their expected variance, and acf is their lag-1 autocorrelation. Here both are inside their intervals. With flicker = false the model of the same fit leaves the flicker FM out and fails:

m0 = clock_model(fit; flicker = false)
innovation_check(kalman_filter(p, m0))
(nis = 1.0704475622336127, nis_bounds = (0.978461102904273, 1.0217701335769125), acf = 0.056737331164725825, acf_bound = 0.0153122186292192, ok = false)

An nis above its interval says that the model has too little noise. On a long record the intervals are narrow, and the test also fails models that predict well (What the states buy). The holdover test asks the more direct question, whether the prediction errors are as large as the filter claims. holdover with a model predicts from many points of the record and returns the RMS error beside the claim:

tau = [10, 100, 1000]
a, b = holdover(p, m; tau), holdover(p, m0; tau)
[a.rms ./ a.claimed  b.rms ./ b.claimed]    # with the states, and without
3×2 Matrix{Float64}:
 1.03237  1.2819
 1.05037  2.45322
 1.01913  5.67909

With the states the errors are those claimed. Without them the filter claims too little, and more so the longer the prediction.

Prediction

holdover_error carries the last estimate forward and returns the standard deviation of the time error that the model gives after each holdover time.

holdover_error(kf, [60.0, 3600.0])    # after a minute and after an hour, in seconds
2-element Vector{Float64}:
 1.1335017053685677e-11
 4.102324887904513e-10

prediction_error measures the same thing inside the record: the RMS difference between the readings and the predictions made a number of samples earlier.

Tuning in place of states

tune_q is a remedy that adds no states. It keeps a model of white and random-walk FM and searches their two levels for the smallest prediction errors at chosen horizons, starting from a fit without flicker terms.

m3 = clock_model(powerlaw_fit(p; alphas = [2, 0, -2]))
t = tune_q(p, m3)
c, d = holdover(p, t.model; tau), holdover(p, m3; tau)
# RMS error over that with the states: tuned, and as fitted
[c.rms ./ a.rms  d.rms ./ a.rms]
3×2 Matrix{Float64}:
 1.0329   1.0075
 1.07502  1.0336
 1.0522   1.25305

Tuning trades the short predictions for the long ones. The tuned levels are no longer those of the noise. tune_q sets their size so that the error claimed one sample ahead is the error made (t.scale is the factor it applied); farther ahead the covariance of the tuned filter, and with it holdover_error, is not to be relied on. In simulation the predictions of tuned models were 5 to 29 % worse than those of models with flicker states, and the errors they claimed from 10 to 1000 s were 1.2 to 2.8 times too small (What the states buy). t.edge says whether the best pair lies on the edge of the grid, where a larger decades is called for.

Simulated clocks

simulate_clock draws a record from a model and returns the true states behind it, so that an estimate can be compared with the truth. The record follows the model exactly, Markov states included, which noise_gen records of power-law noise do only approximately.

truth_model = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-22, q1 = 1e-24, q2 = 1e-30)
q, X = simulate_clock(truth_model, 4096; rng = Xoshiro(2))
kq = kalman_filter(q, truth_model)
err = kq.x[1001:end, 1] .- X[1001:end, 1]
# RMS phase error over the filter's 1σ
sqrt(sum(abs2, err) / length(err)) / sqrt(kq.P[1, 1, end])
1.0007726960751835
SigmaTau.kalman_filter — Function
kalman_filter(data::PhaseData, m::ClockModel; x0=nothing, P0=nothing)
    → KalmanResult

Kalman filter of clock model m over a phase record.

Each sample is a reading $z_k = h^{\mathsf T} X_k + v_k$ of the state $X_k$ of ClockModel: the phase, plus the Markov phase states of a model with flicker PM, plus white noise $v_k$ of variance $R$. From one sample to the next the state moves by $Φ$ (state_transition) and gathers noise of covariance $Q$ (process_noise). The filter is the standard recursion (Särkkä and Svensson (2023), Theorem 6.6):

\[\begin{aligned} X^-_k &= Φ X_{k-1}, & P^-_k &= Φ P_{k-1} Φ^{\mathsf T} + Q, \\ ν_k &= z_k - h^{\mathsf T} X^-_k, & S_k &= h^{\mathsf T} P^-_k h + R, \\ K_k &= P^-_k h / S_k, & X_k &= X^-_k + K_k ν_k . \end{aligned}\]

The covariance is updated in the Joseph form (Stein (2003), Eq. (5.8)), which stays symmetric and positive semidefinite under round-off:

\[P_k = (I - K_k h^{\mathsf T})\, P^-_k\, (I - K_k h^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} + R\, K_k K_k^{\mathsf T} .\]

A NaN sample is a gap: the state is predicted across it without an update.

The filter needs no prior from the caller. It starts from a least-squares line through the first 16 readings (a parabola for a model with drift), with the covariance of that fit multiplied by ten, and with the Markov states at zero with their stationary variances.

Pass the result to kalman_smooth, holdover_error, innovation_check or prediction_error.

Arguments

  • data: the phase record. Its tau0 must be the model's.
  • m: the clock model. A model with no noise at all is refused: the gain would be undefined once the start is used up.

Keywords

  • x0, P0: state and covariance at the first sample, before its reading is used, in place of the default start.

Examples

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> p = PhaseData(2e-9 .* (0:199), 1.0);    # a clock that gains 2 ns each second

julia> m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-20, q1 = 1e-22, q2 = 1e-26);

julia> kf = kalman_filter(p, m)
KalmanResult, 200 samples, 2 states

julia> kf.x[end, 2] ≈ 2e-9
true

SigmaTau.KalmanResult — Type
KalmanResult

Output of kalman_filter or kalman_smooth: the state estimate at every sample, its covariance, and the innovations.

Fields

  • model::ClockModel: Clock model the filter ran on.

  • t::Vector{Float64}: Sample times in seconds from the first sample.

  • x::Matrix{Float64}: State estimates, one row per sample: phase (s), frequency, drift (1/s) for a model with a drift state, then the Markov frequency states, which add to the frequency, and the Markov phase states, which add to the readings.

  • P::Array{Float64, 3}: State covariances, P[:, :, k] at sample k.

  • innovation::Vector{Float64}: Innovations $ν_k$, each reading less its prediction (s); NaN at gaps.

  • innovation_var::Vector{Float64}: Innovation variances $S_k$ (s²); NaN at gaps.

  • smoothed::Bool: Whether x and P are smoothed.

  • xpred::Matrix{Float64}

  • Ppred::Array{Float64, 3}

SigmaTau.kalman_smooth — Function
kalman_smooth(kf::KalmanResult) → KalmanResult

Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother over a filtered run: every estimate then uses the whole record, the samples after it as well as those before.

Running backward from the last sample (Särkkä and Svensson (2023), Theorem 12.2),

\[\begin{aligned} G_k &= P_k Φ^{\mathsf T} (P^-_{k+1})^{-1}, \\ X^s_k &= X_k + G_k (X^s_{k+1} - X^-_{k+1}), \\ P^s_k &= P_k + G_k (P^s_{k+1} - P^-_{k+1})\, G_k^{\mathsf T} , \end{aligned}\]

with $X_k$, $P_k$ the filter's estimates and $X^-_k$, $P^-_k$ its predictions. The innovations of the result are the filter's.

Examples

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-20, q1 = 1e-22, q2 = 1e-26);

julia> kf = kalman_filter(PhaseData(zeros(200), 1.0), m);

julia> ks = kalman_smooth(kf)
Smoothed KalmanResult, 200 samples, 2 states

julia> ks.P[1, 1, 100] < kf.P[1, 1, 100]    # the later samples add information
true

SigmaTau.innovation_check — Function
innovation_check(kf::KalmanResult; confidence=0.95) → NamedTuple

Test of the model against the record, from the innovations of the filter.

Under the model the normalized innovations $e_k = ν_k/\sqrt{S_k}$ are independent, with zero mean and unit variance (Särkkä and Svensson (2023), Theorem 6.6). Two statistics of the $n$ innovations test that:

  • The sum of $e_k^2$ is then χ² distributed with $n$ degrees of freedom, which bounds their mean. A mean above the interval says the noise of the model is too low; below, too high.
  • The lag-1 autocorrelation of $e_k$ then stays within $± z_{1-α/2}/\sqrt{n}$, the band of an uncorrelated sequence (NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, §1.3.3.1). Outside it, a noise type is missing from the model or at the wrong level.
A long record resolves small departures

Both intervals narrow as $1/\sqrt{n}$, so on a long record the test fails a model that departs a little from the noise and still predicts well. A model with flicker states is one, since its Markov processes only approximate a flicker spectrum; What the states buy has measured cases. Compare rms with claimed in holdover before discarding the model.

Keywords

  • confidence: probability that each statistic falls inside its interval under the model.

Returns

A named tuple with

  • nis: mean of $e_k^2$, the normalized innovation squared;
  • nis_bounds: its interval at confidence;
  • acf: lag-1 autocorrelation of $e_k$;
  • acf_bound: the bound on its magnitude at confidence;
  • ok: whether both are inside.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-22, q1 = 1e-24, q2 = 1e-30)
p, _ = simulate_clock(m, 4096; rng = Xoshiro(1))
innovation_check(kalman_filter(p, m))        # the model that made the record
innovation_check(kalman_filter(p, ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-22, q1 = 1e-25)))

SigmaTau.prediction_error — Function
prediction_error(kf::KalmanResult, data::PhaseData, horizons; burnin=0)
    → Vector{Float64}

RMS error, in seconds, of the filter's predictions of the readings horizons samples ahead.

From the estimate at each sample $k$ past burnin, the state is carried to $k + h$ by state_transition, and the reading it predicts is compared with the reading there. The error therefore holds the noise of that reading as well as the clock's. Pairs with a gap at either end are skipped.

Arguments

  • kf: the filter's run over data; not a smoothed one, whose estimates already use the later readings.
  • data: the record the filter ran on.
  • horizons: prediction horizons in samples.

Keywords

  • burnin: samples at the start left out of the error.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-22, q1 = 1e-24, q2 = 1e-30)
p, _ = simulate_clock(m, 20_000; rng = Xoshiro(1))
prediction_error(kalman_filter(p, m), p, [1, 10, 100]; burnin = 1000)

SigmaTau.holdover_error — Function
holdover_error(kf::KalmanResult, tau) → Vector{Float64}

Standard deviation, in seconds, of the clock's time error after running free for each τ in tau (seconds) from the last sample, as the model gives it:

\[σ_x(τ) = \sqrt{\left[Φ(τ)\, P\, Φ(τ)^{\mathsf T} + Q(τ)\right]_{11}} ,\]

with $P$ the covariance of the filter at the last sample. The first term is the error of the last estimate carried forward and the second the noise the clock gathers on the way. It is the error of the clock's phase: the noise of a reading, white or flicker PM, is not in it. holdover measures the same error on the record.

Examples

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-20, q1 = 1e-22, q2 = 1e-26);

julia> kf = kalman_filter(PhaseData(zeros(200), 1.0), m);

julia> round.(holdover_error(kf, [10.0, 100.0, 1000.0]); sigdigits = 3)
3-element Vector{Float64}:
 4.82e-11
 1.63e-10
 2.12e-9

SigmaTau.tune_q — Function
tune_q(data::PhaseData, m::ClockModel; horizons=[10, 100, 1000], decades=2,
       points=9, burnin=length(data.x) ÷ 10) → NamedTuple

Retune the white FM and random-walk FM levels of m for prediction.

The filter is run for every pair of levels on a logarithmic grid of points values that spans decades decades either side of m.q1 and m.q2. The pair is kept whose predictions horizons samples ahead have the smallest sum of the logarithms of their RMS errors (prediction_error).

The predictions of a Kalman filter depend on the ratios of its noise levels and not on their size, so that search cannot set the size. Every level of the model with the best pair is therefore multiplied by one factor, scale: the mean square of that filter's normalized innovations past the burn-in. That is the factor of largest likelihood (Tryon and Jones (1983), Eq. (5.1)); it leaves the predictions as they are and makes the error the filter claims one sample ahead the error it makes.

A noise fit gives the levels that match $σ(τ)$; tuning gives the levels that predict best. The two differ when the record has noise that the model lacks, such as flicker FM. The error the tuned filter claims is then right at one sample and not beyond: in simulation it was wrong by a factor of up to 2.8 at longer holdover times (What the states buy). A model with flicker states, the default of clock_model, carries that noise instead, and predicted better there.

Arguments

  • data: the phase record.
  • m: the model to start from, with q1 > 0 and q2 > 0, which center the grid.

Keywords

  • horizons: prediction horizons in samples. Those longer than the record past the burn-in are dropped.
  • decades: half-width of the grid in decades.
  • points: grid points on each axis; the filter runs points^2 times.
  • burnin: samples at the start left out of the error and of scale.

Returns

A named tuple (model, q1, q2, cost, scale, edge): the tuned model; the two grid axes; the cost on the grid, with one row for each q1 and one column for each q2; the factor on the levels; and whether the best pair lies on the edge of the grid, where the minimum may be outside it and a larger decades is called for.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
h = Dict(0 => 2e-24, -1 => 1e-27, -2 => 1e-32)
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 20_000, 1.0; h, rng = Xoshiro(1))
m = clock_model(powerlaw_fit(p; alphas = [2, 0, -2]))    # without flicker FM
t = tune_q(p, m)
t.model.q1 / m.q1, t.model.q2 / m.q2, t.edge
source
SigmaTau.simulate_clock — Function
simulate_clock(m::ClockModel, N; rng=nothing) → (PhaseData, Matrix{Float64})

A phase record of N samples drawn from clock model m, with the true states behind it, for checking a filter against a known truth.

The clock starts at zero phase, frequency and drift, with its Markov states drawn from their stationary distribution, and each step adds noise of covariance process_noise. A reading is the phase, plus the Markov phase states, plus white noise of the model's reading variance.

Arguments

  • m: the clock model.
  • N: number of samples.

Keywords

  • rng: a random number generator, such as Xoshiro(1), for a reproducible record.

Returns

The record, and the true states with one row per sample and the columns of x in KalmanResult.

Examples

using SigmaTau, Random
m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, R = 1e-22, q1 = 1e-24, q2 = 1e-30)
p, X = simulate_clock(m, 4096; rng = Xoshiro(1))
kf = kalman_filter(p, m)
sqrt(sum(abs2, kf.x[:, 1] .- X[:, 1]) / 4096)    # RMS error of the phase estimate
