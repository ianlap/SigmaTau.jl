Spectra and noise models

Spectra

$S_y(f)$, the spectrum of the fractional frequency, is a one-sided Welch estimate. $S_x(f)$ of the time fluctuations and the phase noise $ℒ(f)$ of a carrier are derived from it, through the response of the first difference that takes phase to frequency. Each is returned as a SpectralResult. Conventions gives their units and the relations between them, and Plotting and tables draws them.

SigmaTau.psd_y — Function
psd_y(data; nperseg=nothing, noverlap=nothing, taper=:hann) → SpectralResult

One-sided power spectral density of the fractional frequency, $S_y(f)$ in 1/Hz (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eq. (A.8)), estimated by Welch's method (Welch (1967)).

The record is cut into $K$ overlapping segments of $L$ samples. Each segment has its mean removed and is multiplied by the taper $w$, and the squared magnitudes of the segments' discrete Fourier transforms $Y^{(j)}$ are averaged:

\[\hat S_y(f_k) = \frac{2τ_0}{K \sum_{i=1}^{L} w_i^2} \sum_{j=1}^{K} \left| Y^{(j)}_k \right|^2, \qquad f_k = \frac{k}{Lτ_0}, \quad k = 0, \ldots, \lfloor L/2 \rfloor .\]

The factor 2 folds the negative frequencies into the one-sided density. It is left out at $f = 0$ and, for even $L$, at the Nyquist frequency $1/(2τ_0)$. With this scaling the density summed over the bins, times the bin width $1/(Lτ_0)$, approximates the variance of $y$.

The mean removed from a segment is the one weighted by the taper, $\sum_i w_i y_i / \sum_i w_i$. The estimate is then zero at $f = 0$, and a frequency offset leaves no trace in it. Segments and scaling are those of scipy.signal.welch with scaling="density", whose detrend="constant" removes the plain mean instead. With the Hann and Hamming tapers the two estimates differ at $f = 0$ and in the first bin, $1/(Lτ_0)$, and are equal from the second bin on, where the validation suite compares them.

Arguments

  • data: a FrequencyData record, or a PhaseData record, which is differenced to frequency first. A missing sample makes the whole spectrum NaN: fill gaps first with fillgaps.

Keywords

  • nperseg: segment length $L$, in samples. The default is the largest power of two up to a quarter of the record, at least 16 and no longer than the record. A longer segment resolves lower frequencies; a shorter one averages more segments and scatters less.
  • noverlap: samples shared by successive segments; half a segment by default.
  • taper=:hann: :hann, :hamming or :rectangular.
Lowest bins, tapers and drift

The taper spreads each frequency over its neighbors, and the first bin shares the main lobe of the taper with the mean that was removed. In simulation with the Hann taper it reads between 0.56 times the spectrum, on flicker FM, and 1.08 times, on white PM, at any segment length, and should not be read. From the second bin on, the expected value of the Hann estimate is the spectrum itself on flicker PM and white FM. It is 8 % high at the second bin and 4 % at the third on white PM, 11 % and 4 % on flicker FM, and on random-walk FM 48 %, 15 % and 7 % high at the second to fourth bins and within 2 % from the eighth.

The side lobes of the Hamming and rectangular tapers fall slowly, and they carry the top of a PM spectrum down into the lowest bins, the more so the longer the segment. With $L = 1024$ on white PM the Hamming estimate is 28 % high at the second bin and 7 % at the fourth, and the rectangular one is high by 20 % or more up to the 16th bin. The rectangular taper is also a factor of 2 high at every bin on random-walk FM.

A linear frequency drift is not removed, and it raises the lowest bins. Take it out first with detrend: method=:quadratic for a phase record, :linear for a frequency record.

Examples

A sinusoid of unit amplitude has the variance 1/2:

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> y = FrequencyData([sinpi(k / 4) for k in 0:1023], 1.0);

julia> s = psd_y(y);

julia> (s.nperseg, s.noverlap, length(s.freq))
(256, 128, 129)

julia> sum(s.psd) * (s.freq[2] - s.freq[1]) ≈ 0.5
true

julia> s.freq[argmax(s.psd)]
0.125

SigmaTau.psd_x — Function
psd_x(data; nperseg=nothing, noverlap=nothing, taper=:hann) → SpectralResult

One-sided power spectral density of the time fluctuations, $S_x(f)$ in s²/Hz (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eq. (A.14)): the estimate of $S_y(f)$ by psd_y, divided by the response of the first difference that takes phase to frequency.

\[\hat S_x(f_k) = \frac{τ_0^2}{4 \sin^2(π f_k τ_0)}\, \hat S_y(f_k), \qquad k = 1, \ldots, \lfloor L/2 \rfloor .\]

The two densities are related by $S_y(f) = (2πf)^2 S_x(f)$ (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eq. (A.16)), and the response of the first difference over $τ_0$ is $(2πf)^2$ well below the Nyquist frequency. The value at $f = 0$ is NaN.

The spectrum is not estimated on the phase itself, because the phase spectrum of FM noise is steep, $f^{-2}$ for white FM to $f^{-4}$ for random-walk FM, and what a taper leaks from the lowest frequencies then exceeds the spectrum. In simulation a Welch estimate on the phase, with the Hann taper, reads 3.5 times high at the second bin on flicker FM and more than 100 times on random-walk FM, and a frequency offset swamps every low bin. The spectrum of the frequency is less steep by $f^2$, and a frequency offset is only its mean. At each bin the estimate is high or low by the factor that psd_y is, and the note there on the lowest bins, the tapers and drift applies.

Arguments

  • data: a PhaseData or FrequencyData record. A missing sample makes the whole spectrum NaN, as in psd_y.

Keywords

Those of psd_y, with one difference. A segment of nperseg frequency samples spans nperseg + 1 phase samples, and the default is the largest power of two up to a quarter of the number of phase samples, at least 16. psd_y counts the frequency samples, one fewer, so for a phase record of $2^j ≥ 128$ samples its default segment is half as long.

source
SigmaTau.ssb_phase_noise — Function
ssb_phase_noise(data; f_carrier, nperseg=nothing, noverlap=nothing,
                taper=:hann) → SpectralResult

Phase noise $ℒ(f)$ in dBc/Hz of a signal at the carrier frequency f_carrier, in Hz, from its phase or frequency record.

The time spectrum of psd_x gives the phase spectrum in rad²/Hz, $S_φ(f) = (2πν_0)^2 S_x(f)$ with $ν_0$ the carrier frequency, and the phase noise is half of it, $ℒ(f) = S_φ(f)/2$, reported as $10 \log_{10} ℒ(f)$ (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eqs. (A.13) and (A.15)). So defined, $ℒ(f)$ is the single-sideband noise power relative to the carrier only for small phase modulation (clause 4 of the standard).

The result runs over the offset frequencies from $1/(Lτ_0)$ to the Nyquist frequency $1/(2τ_0)$; the bin at $f = 0$ is dropped. The other keywords are those of psd_x, and the note of psd_y on the lowest bins, the tapers and drift applies: the first point of the result is its first bin.

SigmaTau.integrated_jitter — Function
integrated_jitter(s::SpectralResult; f_carrier, band=nothing) → NamedTuple
integrated_jitter(f, S; quantity=:L, f_carrier, band=nothing) → NamedTuple

Phase jitter, time jitter and residual FM of a signal at the carrier frequency f_carrier, in Hz: its phase spectrum $S_φ(f)$ integrated over the band $f_1$ to $f_2$ of offset frequencies. The result has the fields

  • phase: $Δφ = \left(\int_{f_1}^{f_2} S_φ(f)\,df\right)^{1/2}$, in radians rms (IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eq. (A.17)).
  • time: $Δφ/(2πν_0)$, in seconds rms, the same integral of $S_x(f)$.
  • residual_fm: $Δf = \left(\int_{f_1}^{f_2} f^2 S_φ(f)\,df\right)^{1/2}$, in hertz rms.

Between neighboring points the spectrum is taken as a power law, $S_φ(f) = K f^x$, a straight line on a log-log plot, and each segment is integrated in closed form (SP1065 §5.2.19, Eqs. (38) to (43)).

Arguments

  • s: a result of ssb_phase_noise, psd_x or psd_y.
  • f, S: spectrum points, such as the $ℒ(f)$ table of a phase-noise analyzer or a data sheet. f is in Hz and increases.

Keywords

  • f_carrier: the carrier frequency $ν_0$, in Hz.
  • band=nothing: the limits (f₁, f₂), in Hz, inside the range of the spectrum. The whole spectrum is integrated by default.
  • quantity=:L: what S holds: :L, $ℒ(f)$ in dBc/Hz, with $S_φ = 2ℒ$; :Sx, $S_x(f)$ in s²/Hz; or :Sy, $S_y(f)$ in 1/Hz, converted with $(2πf)^2$. A psd_y result is converted with the response of the first difference instead (see psd_x).

Points at $f = 0$ and points without a positive spectrum are left out.

This is jitter as the integral of the phase noise over a band. Howe and Tasset (2003) treat jitter as a function of the delay $τ$, the rms of $x(t+τ) - x(t)$, which tierms estimates from a phase record.

Examples

A phase noise that falls by 10 dB per decade, $x = -1$, on a 10 MHz carrier; the results are in rad, ps and Hz:

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> j = integrated_jitter([1e3, 1e4, 1e5], [-100.0, -110.0, -120.0];
                             f_carrier=10e6);

julia> r4(v) = round(v; sigdigits=4);

julia> r4(j.phase), r4(j.time * 1e12), r4(j.residual_fm)
(0.0009597, 15.27, 31.62)

Power-law model

The model $S_y(f) = \sum_α h_α f^α$ links the spectra and the deviations. powerlaw_fit fits the $h_α$ to the deviation of a record or to a spectrum, and powerlaw_sigma and powerlaw_psd give the deviation and the spectra of a model. Power-law noise has the conversions.

The spectrum can be a table of points, such as the phase noise on a data sheet:

using SigmaTau

# ℒ(f) in dBc/Hz of a 10 MHz oscillator, at offsets from 1 Hz to 10 kHz
f = powerlaw_fit([1, 10, 100, 1e3, 1e4], [-100, -130, -150, -158, -160];
                 quantity = :L, f_carrier = 10e6)
PowerLawFit to L (5 points)
  h2   WHPM  1.874e-30
  h1   FLPM  1.252e-27
  h0   WHFM  4.204e-26
  h-1  FLFM  1.397e-24
  h-2  RWFM  5.602e-25

The Allan deviation that this model gives at 1 ms, 10 ms and 0.1 s, in a record sampled every millisecond:

powerlaw_sigma(f, [0.001, 0.01, 0.1]; tau0 = 1e-3)
3-element Vector{Float64}:
 1.6253315387922985e-11
 2.940535948150751e-12
 1.6070264598749482e-12

The table says nothing of the noise below 1 Hz, which sets the deviation at averaging times of a second and more, so the model is evaluated only where the table supports it.

SigmaTau.powerlaw_fit — Function
powerlaw_fit(data, m_values=Int[]; deviation=:mhdev, alphas=2:-1:-2) → PowerLawFit
powerlaw_fit(s::SpectralResult; alphas=2:-1:-2, f_carrier=nothing) → PowerLawFit
powerlaw_fit(f, S; quantity=:Sy, alphas=2:-1:-2, f_carrier=nothing) → PowerLawFit

Coefficients $h_α$ of the power-law model $S_y(f) = \sum_α h_α f^α$, fitted by nonnegative least squares to the deviation of a record or to a spectrum.

From a record

The deviation of data, a PhaseData or FrequencyData record that may have gaps, is computed without bias correction on the octave grid, or at m_values. Each component enters with the variance it has in that estimator on a record sampled at $τ_0$ (see powerlaw_sigma). That response is exact from $m = 1$, so every point is fitted, and it separates white PM from flicker PM even under ADEV, where both fall close to $τ^{-2}$.

A point is weighted by $ν/(2σ^4)$, the inverse variance of a $χ^2$-distributed estimate, with $σ^2$ from the model and the EDF $ν$ from the point's dominant component and its number of terms. An averaging time at which gaps cost terms therefore counts for less.

From a spectrum

The spectrum is a result of psd_y, psd_x or ssb_phase_noise, or points f, S such as the phase-noise table of a data sheet. A Welch estimate is fitted with the spectrum of a sampled record, $h_α (\sin(πfτ_0)/(πτ_0))^α$, and points with $h_α f^α$. Every point has the same fractional weight, and a point at $f = 0$ is left out.

Keywords

  • deviation=:mhdev: the estimator fitted, :adev, :mdev, :hdev or :mhdev.
  • alphas=2:-1:-2: the exponents fitted. The Allan pair takes $α$ from 2 to −2. The Hadamard pair takes it to −4 and is insensitive to linear frequency drift.
  • quantity=:Sy: what S holds: :Sy (1/Hz), :Sx (s²/Hz) or :L (dBc/Hz).
  • f_carrier: the carrier frequency in Hz, needed for $ℒ(f)$.
Reading the result

A coefficient cannot be negative, so a component the record does not have comes out small and positive, at the expense of its neighbors. In simulation (white PM, white FM and random-walk FM, 65 536 samples) the components present were recovered about 2 % low with a scatter of 2 to 3 %, except $h_{-2}$, which rests on the few longest averaging times and scattered by 20 %. Compare fit with y before trusting a small coefficient.

noise_id_nnls fits the asymptotic power laws of the variance to name the noise types present; this fit gives the calibrated $h_α$.

Examples

p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 65536, 1.0; h=Dict(2 => 1e-22, 0 => 1e-24, -2 => 1e-30))
f = powerlaw_fit(p)                 # from MHDEV
powerlaw_sigma(f, [1.0, 100.0])     # the ADEV the model gives at 1 s and 100 s
source

SigmaTau.powerlaw_sigma — Function
powerlaw_sigma(h, tau; deviation=:adev, tau0) → Vector{Float64}

Deviation $σ(τ)$ that the power-law model $S_y(f) = \sum_α h_α f^α$ gives in an estimator, for a record sampled at tau0.

The model is taken as the spectrum of a sampled record, $h_α (\sin(πfτ_0)/(πτ_0))^α$ up to the Nyquist frequency $f_h = 1/(2τ_0)$. That is the spectrum noise_gen produces, and it is $h_α f^α$ well below $f_h$. It is integrated through the transfer function of the estimator at $τ = mτ_0$:

\[σ^2(τ) = \int_0^{f_h} S_y(f)\,\lvert H_m(f) \rvert^2\,df .\]

deviation$\lvert H_m(f) \rvert^2$, with $θ = πfτ_0$
:adev$2\sin^4(mθ)/(m^2\sin^2θ)$
:mdev$2\sin^6(mθ)/(m^4\sin^4θ)$
:hdev$(8/3)\sin^6(mθ)/(m^2\sin^2θ)$
:mhdev$(8/3)\sin^8(mθ)/(m^4\sin^4θ)$

For ADEV at $m ≫ 1$ this is the usual conversion (SP1065 §7.1, Eq. (67); IEEE Std 1139-2022, Eq. (B.4)): $h_0/(2τ)$ for white FM, $2\ln 2\,h_{-1}$ for flicker FM and $(2π^2/3)\,h_{-2}\,τ$ for random-walk FM. The white FM term and the white PM term, $3 f_h h_2/(4π^2 τ^2)$, hold at every $m$. At $τ_0$ the sampled record is above the conversion by 44 % in variance on flicker FM and 50 % on random-walk FM, and within 1 % from $16τ_0$. On flicker PM it is 19 % above at $τ_0$ and still 4 % above at $64τ_0$.

Arguments

  • h: a PowerLawFit, a NoiseFit, a ClockModel, or a Dict of $h_α$ keyed by $α$ or by noise type, as in noise_gen.
  • tau: averaging times in seconds, each a whole multiple of tau0.

Keywords

  • deviation=:adev: :adev, :mdev, :hdev or :mhdev.
  • tau0: the sample interval in seconds. A fit or a clock model carries its own.

Examples

White FM with random-walk FM, at 1 s, 100 s and 10⁴ s:

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> h = Dict(:WHFM => 2e-24, :RWFM => 1e-30);

julia> print(round.(powerlaw_sigma(h, [1.0, 100.0, 1e4]; tau0=1.0); sigdigits=4))
[1.0e-12, 1.032e-13, 2.567e-13]

A model built by hand

noise_fit builds the model one noise type at a time. Each component has the slope of its noise type and is fitted over a chosen range of the $τ$ grid to what the other components leave. It serves where the noise types and their ranges are known, and where powerlaw_fit spends a component on a bump of the curve.

using Random

# white PM, white FM and random-walk FM
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 8192, 1.0; rng = Xoshiro(5),
              sigma1 = Dict(2 => 2e-12, 0 => 1e-12, -2 => 3e-14))
m = noise_fit(mhdev(p))
add_component!(m, :WHPM, 1:2)      # white PM, fitted at the first two τ
add_component!(m, :WHFM, 5:8)
NoiseFit to mhdev (2 components)
  h2   WHPM  9.031e-23  τ 1–2 s
  h0   WHFM  2.768e-24  τ 16–128 s

noise_residual returns the variance that the model leaves at each $τ$, and undo! takes the last component back.

SigmaTau.noise_fit — Function
noise_fit(r::StabilityResult) → NoiseFit

Start an interactive power-law fit on r, an ADEV, MDEV, HDEV or MHDEV result or one of their total forms (which are fitted with the parent's response). Add components with add_component! and take the last one back with undo!.

Each component's σ²(τ) is the exact response of a sampled record, as in powerlaw_fit. Its level is the weighted mean of log(residual / response) over its own τ range, the residual being the measured σ² less every other component, with the slope fixed and each point weighted by its confidence interval, 1/log(hi/lo)². Without intervals the points weigh the same. After each change every level is refitted against the others until they settle, so the model does not depend on the order the components were added in, and a component is not inflated by a neighbour that was added after it.

Examples

using SigmaTau
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 65536, 1.0; h = Dict(2 => 1e-22, 0 => 1e-24, -2 => 1e-30))
f = noise_fit(mhdev(p))
add_component!(f, :WHPM, 1:3)      # white PM on the first three τ
add_component!(f, :WHFM, 4:9)
add_component!(f, :RWFM, 11:14)
undo!(f)                           # take random-walk FM back out
clock_model(f)
source

Clock model

clock_model turns a power-law model into the ClockModel on which the Kalman filter and the time scales run. White, random-walk and random-run FM drive the phase, the frequency and the drift, and PM noise becomes the variance of a reading. Flicker FM and flicker PM each become a set of Markov states. With flicker = false they do not: flicker FM is left out, and the model then holds less noise than the fit found. The clock model derives the mapping and measures what the states change.

clock_model(m)
2-state ClockModel, τ₀ = 1.0 s
  R  1.144e-24 s²
  q1 1.384e-24 s
  q2 0 1/s
SigmaTau.ClockModel — Type
ClockModel(; tau0, R=0, q1=0, q2=0, q3=0, drift=q3 > 0, flicker_fm=0,
           flicker_pm=0, tau_max=1e6 * tau0, fm_band=nothing, pm_band=nothing)

State-space model of a clock read every tau0 seconds: phase, frequency and, with drift = true, frequency drift driven by white noises, with optional Markov states for flicker noise.

The phase $x$, frequency $y$ and drift $d$ follow the three-state model of Zucca and Tavella (2015), Eq. (1) without its deterministic terms,

\[dx = y\,dt + \sqrt{q_1}\,dW_1, \qquad dy = d\,dt + \sqrt{q_2}\,dW_2, \qquad dd = \sqrt{q_3}\,dW_3 ,\]

with independent Wiener processes $W_i$; $q_1$, $q_2$ and $q_3$ are the squares of that paper's diffusion coefficients. A reading is the phase plus white noise of variance $R$. The levels stand for the coefficients of $S_y(f) = \sum_α h_α f^α$ as follows, with the white PM taken up to the Nyquist frequency $1/(2τ_0)$:

FieldNoiseLevelUnit
Rwhite PM of a reading$h_2 = 8π^2 τ_0 R$s²
q1white FM$h_0 = 2 q_1$s
q2random-walk FM$h_{-2} = q_2/(2π^2)$1/s
q3random-run FM$h_{-4} = q_3/(8π^4)$1/s³
flicker_fmflicker FM$h_{-1}$–
flicker_pmflicker PM of a reading$h_1$s²

R and flicker_pm describe the readings; q1 to q3 and flicker_fm are the clock's own noise, the process noise of process_noise. clock_model builds a model from a noise fit, and powerlaw_sigma and powerlaw_psd take a model and return the deviation or the spectrum of the power law it stands for.

With flicker_fm > 0 the state gains Markov frequency processes that add to the frequency: the flicker FM model of Davis et al. (2005), Eqs. (2.1)–(2.10), independent first-order processes of equal variance whose time constants rise geometrically. The paper spaces them by a factor of 8 or 16; here they are a decade apart, each of variance $h_{-1} \ln 10$. With flicker_pm > 0 the state gains Markov phase processes of variance $h_1 \ln 10/(4π^2)$ that add to each reading and not to the phase: measurement noise with memory, which a filter estimates and keeps out of the phase. The paper treats flicker FM only; using its construction on the readings is this package's choice.

The states are placed where the flicker matters. By default fm_band and pm_band are the first and last τ, between τ₀ and tau_max, at which that flicker holds at least a quarter of the model's Allan variance. The time constants start at the lower end of the band and rise by decades until one reaches the upper end, which makes $1 + ⌈\log_{10}(τ_2/τ_1)⌉$ states for a band from $τ_1$ to $τ_2$. A flicker that never holds a quarter gets no states. What the states leave out at τ₀ is added as white noise, to q1 for flicker FM and to R for flicker PM, so that the model's Allan variance at τ₀ is that of the sampled flicker. Flicker states gives the accuracy of the approximation.

Keywords

  • tau0: sample interval in seconds.
  • R: white PM variance of a reading, in s².
  • q1, q2, q3: white FM, random-walk FM and random-run FM levels.
  • drift: whether the state carries a frequency drift; q3 > 0 needs it.
  • flicker_fm, flicker_pm: flicker levels $h_{-1}$ and $h_1$.
  • tau_max: longest τ, in seconds, at which the default bands are looked for.
  • fm_band, pm_band: first and last τ, in seconds, that the Markov states span, in place of the default; (0, 0) for no states.

Fields

  • tau0::Float64: Sample interval in seconds, to which R refers.

  • R::Float64: White PM variance of a reading (s²).

  • q1::Float64: White FM level $q_1$ (s).

  • q2::Float64: Random-walk FM level $q_2$ (1/s).

  • q3::Float64: Random-run FM level $q_3$ (1/s³).

  • drift::Bool: Whether the state carries a frequency drift.

  • flicker_fm::Float64: Flicker FM level $h_{-1}$.

  • fm_band::Tuple{Float64, Float64}: First and last τ (s) that the Markov frequency states span; (0, 0) for none.

  • flicker_pm::Float64: Flicker PM level $h_1$ (s²).

  • pm_band::Tuple{Float64, Float64}: First and last τ (s) that the Markov phase states span; (0, 0) for none.

SigmaTau.clock_model — Function
clock_model(fit; drift, flicker=true, tau_max, fm_band, pm_band) → ClockModel
clock_model(h::AbstractDict; tau0, drift, flicker=true, tau_max, fm_band,
            pm_band) → ClockModel

The ClockModel of a power-law noise model.

White FM, random-walk FM and random-run FM (α = 0, −2, −4) become q1, q2 and q3, and white PM (α = 2) becomes R, by the relations listed under ClockModel. The flicker noises have no finite state, and flicker decides what becomes of them.

By default, flicker FM and flicker PM (α = −1, 1) are kept as the Markov states of ClockModel, across the τ where each holds a quarter of the model's Allan variance, and R is the white PM alone. A fit without flicker gives the same model with either setting.

With flicker = false, flicker FM is left out and flicker PM is folded into R. The model then has two states, or three with drift, as the :stein time scale of timescale needs, and it has less noise than the fit: in simulation the filter of a clock on its flicker floor claimed several times less than the error it made (What the states buy). The fold rests on what a filter does with PM: it averages the PM that is faster than its own response and follows the rest as if it were the clock. R stands for the part it averages, as the measurement variance of Van Dierendonck et al. (1984), Eqs. (68)–(69) is the phase noise that a tracking loop does not follow:

\[R = \frac{(m τ_0)^2\, σ_{\mathrm{PM}}^2(m τ_0)}{3} ,\]

with $σ_{\mathrm{PM}}^2$ the Allan variance of the white and flicker PM together and $m$ the last lag, up to 65 536, at which it exceeds that of the FM terms (the Hadamard variances are compared when the model has α = −3 or −4). For white PM alone this is $h_2/(8π^2 τ_0)$ at every lag. The paper gives the principle; the formula is this package's.

Flicker-walk FM (α = −3) is left out with either setting. powerlaw_sigma(model, τ) beside powerlaw_sigma(fit, τ) shows what a model leaves out at the τ of interest.

Arguments

  • fit: a PowerLawFit that has a sample interval, or a NoiseFit.
  • h: a Dict of $h_α$ keyed by $α$ or by noise type, as in powerlaw_sigma.

Keywords

  • tau0: sample interval in seconds; a fit carries its own.
  • drift: whether the model has a drift state; by default, whether $h_{-4}$ is positive.
  • flicker: keep flicker FM and flicker PM as Markov states; false leaves flicker FM out and folds flicker PM into R.
  • tau_max, fm_band, pm_band: passed to ClockModel. From a PowerLawFit of a deviation curve, tau_max defaults to its longest τ.

Examples

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> h = Dict(2 => 8π^2 * 1e-22, 0 => 2e-24, -2 => 1e-30 / 2π^2);

julia> m = clock_model(h; tau0 = 1.0);

julia> (m.R, m.q1, m.q2) .≈ (1e-22, 1e-24, 1e-30)
(true, true, true)

From a record, with the flicker that the fit finds kept as states:

SigmaTau.state_transition — Function
state_transition(m::ClockModel, δ) → Matrix{Float64}

Matrix $Φ(δ)$ that carries the state of m over a step of δ seconds.

For phase, frequency and drift,

\[Φ(δ) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & δ & δ^2/2 \\ 0 & 1 & δ \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ,\]

or its upper-left 2 × 2 block without drift. Each Markov state of time constant $T$ adds a row and a column after these, the frequency states first: it decays by $a = e^{-δ/T}$, and a frequency state moves the phase by $T(1 - a)$ times itself (Davis et al. (2005), Eqs. (2.7) and (3.3)).

SigmaTau.process_noise — Function
process_noise(m::ClockModel, δ) → Matrix{Float64}

Covariance $Q(δ)$ of the noise that the state of m gathers over a step of δ seconds.

The white noises of the model, integrated over the step, give (Greenhall (2003), Eq. (5); Zucca and Tavella (2015), Eq. (3))

\[Q(δ) = \begin{pmatrix} q_1 δ + q_2 δ^3/3 + q_3 δ^5/20 & q_2 δ^2/2 + q_3 δ^4/8 & q_3 δ^3/6 \\ q_2 δ^2/2 + q_3 δ^4/8 & q_2 δ + q_3 δ^3/3 & q_3 δ^2/2 \\ q_3 δ^3/6 & q_3 δ^2/2 & q_3 δ \end{pmatrix} ,\]

or its upper-left 2 × 2 block without drift. Steps compose: $Q(a + b) = Φ(b)\,Q(a)\,Φ(b)^{\mathsf T} + Q(b)$, with $Φ$ from state_transition.

A Markov frequency state $j$ of time constant $T$ and variance $σ^2$, with $a = e^{-δ/T}$, adds (Davis et al. (2005), Eqs. (2.8)–(2.9) and (3.4))

\[Q_{11} \mathrel{+}= σ^2 T^2 \left(2δ/T - 3 + 4a - a^2\right), \qquad Q_{1j} = σ^2 T (1 - a)^2, \qquad Q_{jj} = σ^2 (1 - a^2) ,\]

and a Markov phase state only its own $Q_{jj} = σ^2 (1 - a^2)$. The white FM that stands for the flicker FM the states leave out at $τ_0$ is added to $q_1$.

Examples

julia> using SigmaTau

julia> m = ClockModel(; tau0 = 1.0, q1 = 1e-24, q2 = 3e-30);

julia> process_noise(m, 10.0) ≈ [1e-23+1e-27 1.5e-28; 1.5e-28 3e-29]
true

julia> Φ, Q = state_transition(m, 5.0), process_noise(m, 5.0);

julia> process_noise(m, 10.0) ≈ Φ * Q * Φ' + Q
true

Cross-spectra

cross_psd_y and cross_psd_x average the cross-spectrum of two simultaneous records that share a device and nothing else, so that the noise of the two channels averages toward zero. Two-channel measurements says when the method applies and what it cannot remove. cross_adev is its counterpart in the time domain.

SigmaTau.cross_psd_y — Function
cross_psd_y(a, b; nperseg=nothing, noverlap=nothing,
            taper=:hann) → CrossSpectralResult

Cross power spectral density of the fractional frequency of two simultaneous records of one device, in 1/Hz.

The estimate is Welch's average of cross-periodograms (Welch (1967), p. 73): that of psd_y with the product $A_k^* B_k$ of the two channels' transforms in place of $|Y_k|^2$. Its real part is reported (Baudiquez et al. (2020)).

Noise present in both channels survives the average. Noise in only one, such as each reference or each channel's electronics, averages toward zero and leaves the residual given in the floor field of the result. A device measured against two independent references shows its own spectrum below the noise of either reference, and one signal split into two channels shows the noise of the source below the channels' own.

Baudiquez et al. (2020) derive the distribution of the estimate, a variance-gamma distribution, and from it an upper limit for a spectrum that is not resolved; Baudiquez et al. (2022) extend it to more than two instruments. Neither is implemented here.

Arguments

  • a, b: two PhaseData or two FrequencyData records with the same sample interval and length, and no missing samples.

The keywords are those of psd_y.

Fields
