Choosing an estimator

The estimator follows from the question asked of the record and from what the record contains. The table gives the short answer and the sections below it the reasons, after the advice of SP1065 §5.2 and §10.13. With no question yet, run stability, which computes adev, mdev, hdev and mhdev, and compare the curves.

estimators returns the list as data, and deviation runs an estimator chosen by name.

Drift

A linear frequency drift $D$ adds $D^2τ^2/2$ to the Allan variance and hides the noise at long averaging times (Power-law noise). There are two ways around it.

The Hadamard family (hdev, mhdev, htdev, htotdev, mhtotdev) takes a third difference of the phase, to which a linear frequency drift contributes nothing. It also converges on flicker-walk and random-run FM, where the Allan family does not. The price is confidence: at the same averaging time a Hadamard deviation has fewer degrees of freedom than the Allan deviation (SP1065 §5.2).

The other way is to estimate the drift, remove it, and use the Allan family on what remains. SP1065 advises it as the usual course: the stability plot shows the noise, and the drift is analyzed separately (§5.19 and §11.2).

d = drift_fit(p)                          # the drift and its model
adev(detrend(p; method = :quadratic))     # the noise that remains

Gaps, outliers and drift covers both functions.

Phase noise

White and flicker PM both give the Allan deviation a slope near $τ^{-1}$. The modified deviations average the phase over $τ$ before differencing and fall as $τ^{-3/2}$ on white PM and $τ^{-1}$ on flicker PM. Use mdev, or mhdev when the record also drifts, wherever a phase noise is to be identified or its level read off.

pdev has the same two slopes. It estimates each frequency by linear regression on the phase, the best estimate in white phase noise, which makes it the more efficient of the two and lets it identify a noise type from a shorter record (IEEE Std 1139-2022, A.3.4).

Long averaging times

At the longest averaging times an estimator rests on a few terms. The equivalent degrees of freedom (EDF) say how few. Here they are for three estimators at $τ ≈ T/32$, $T/8$ and $T/4$ on white FM:

using SigmaTau, Random
p = noise_gen(PhaseData, 4096, 1.0; sigma1 = Dict(0 => 1e-12), rng = Xoshiro(1))
edf(est, m) = round.(est(p, m; noise = :WHFM).edf; digits = 1)

(adev = edf(adev, [128, 512, 1024]), totdev = edf(totdev, [128, 512, 1024]),
 theo1 = edf(theo1, [170, 682, 1366]))
(adev = [45.8, 9.8, 4.0], totdev = [48.0, 12.0, 6.0], theo1 = [128.2, 29.7, 13.3])

The total estimators extend the record by reflection at both ends, which adds half again to the EDF of the Allan deviation at a quarter of the record. Each keeps the properties of the deviation it extends: totdev goes with adev, mtotdev with mdev, htotdev with hdev and mhtotdev with mhdev. They are biased by an amount that depends on the noise type, which the package identifies and corrects for (Bias correction). State the noise type with noise= when it is known, because the label is least certain where these estimators are most useful.

Thêo1 has about three times the EDF of the Allan deviation and reaches three quarters of the record. theo1 removes its bias by a ratio measured on the record, which holds on FM noise of a single type and adds a scatter of its own, about 15 % in deviation. It reads wrong where PM noise dominates and where the noise type changes within the record (Bias correction has the sizes). At short averaging times that scatter leaves it less precise than the Allan deviation, so for a whole curve use theoh, which joins the Allan deviation at short averaging times to the corrected Thêo1 at long ones.

Time error

tdev, htdev and ttotdev are the modified deviations as a time, in seconds: the usual measure of a time-transfer link or a network clock. mtie gives the largest peak-to-peak time error within any interval of length $τ$, and tierms the rms change of the phase over $τ$, which includes any frequency offset. MTIE is a peak, not an average: a single outlier sets it, and it has no confidence interval. The ITU-T masks for network clocks limit MTIE and TDEV, and mask_check compares a result with one of them.

Averaging times

Octave spacing, the default, gives points evenly spaced on a logarithmic axis, and Decade gives round values. AllTaus computes every averaging factor. It shows a periodic disturbance as a ripple in the curve (SP1065 §5.23), and costs a call for each factor.

adev(p, AllTaus)

Counter readings

What a deviation of frequency readings estimates depends on how the counter averaged each reading (IEEE Std 1139-2022, A.3.1.2). A Π counter weights the interval uniformly, and the two-sample deviation of its readings is the Allan deviation. A Λ counter weights it with a triangle and an Ω counter with a parabola. The two-sample deviation of their readings is a modified Allan deviation and a parabolic deviation, whatever the function that computed it is called. Phase samples avoid the question. The standard also prefers them where there is dead time between the readings, which this package does not correct for.

What each estimator needs

EstimatorsGapsCost for each $τ$
adev, mdev, tdev, hdev, mhdev, htdevskipped$O(N)$
mtie, tiermsskipped$O(N)$
pdevmust be filled$O(N)$
totdevmust be filled$O(N)$
mtotdev, ttotdev, htotdev, mhtotdevmust be filled$O(Nm)$, on all threads
theo1, theohmust be filled$O(Nm)$, on all threads
dadev, dhdevmust be filledone deviation for each window

An estimator that skips gaps leaves out the terms that touch a missing sample. The others need fillgaps first, which changes the noise inside each gap, so prefer an estimator that skips them when the record has many (Gaps, outliers and drift). Start Julia with --threads=auto for the last rows of the table: on a long record the modified total and Thêo1 estimators are the only ones that take noticeable time.
