Ensemble

The Ensemble page works on a group of clocks that were measured against one reference, in two views. Time scale forms a time scale from them: a weighted combination of the clocks that is meant to be more stable than any one of them. It shows the stability of the scale, the stability of each clock against it and the weight of each clock. N-cornered hat separates the stability of each clock from the comparisons, without a scale (The N-cornered hat).

The clocks

The page takes its clocks from the first of these that it finds:

  1. the clock columns of one loaded file of plain columns, two or more, read at the same instants against the same reference;
  2. clocks simulated together on the Data or Synthesis page;
  3. four simulated clocks of its own, of 16384 samples at 1 s.

A note in the side panel says which clocks are in use. A clock chosen as the Reference in the list of clocks is left out of the scale, and so is a clock removed with ×. A hidden clock stays in. The hat keeps the clock chosen as the Reference: it works on the difference of every pair of clocks, which that choice does not change.

Both views take the columns as the import dialog reads them. The drift removal, the gap filling, the outlier removal and the analysis range of the Data page do not apply here.

Where phase columns have missing samples, the scale is formed without the clock at those samples (Clocks that drop out and return). Greenhall, Jones–Tryon and Stein first fit a model to each clock, from the samples at which it is read with two others. A file that leaves a clock too few of them stops the fit; AT1, AT2 and EWFA form a scale from it. Frequency columns with missing samples cannot be read.

AlgorithmScale
AT1Weighs each clock by the inverse of the variance of its prediction error, which the scale estimates as it runs (Weiss and Weissert (1991))
AT2AT1 with the frequency of each clock followed by a Kalman filter, from the same paper. With Frequency steps, a clock whose frequency steps is left out for a time. Best forms the scale again from the step on, and Real keeps the values already issued and corrects those that follow
EWFAWeighs every clock the same (Kettering (2023), §3.1)
GreenhallOne Kalman filter over all the clocks, with the covariance reduction of Greenhall (2003), read at the pivot clock
Jones–TryonThe same filter without the reduction (Jones and Tryon (1983)). It defines no weights
SteinThe scale of Stein (2003), in the form that Levine (2012) describes as KAS-2: a Kalman filter on the difference of each clock from the pivot, with weights for phase, frequency and aging. The weights shown are those of the phase

timescale describes each algorithm, its equations and where it departs from its source. The three Kalman scales need three or more clocks. They fit a model to each clock from the comparisons alone, as clock_models does.

The model of a clock has white FM and random-walk FM, with the PM as the noise of its readings. With Flicker states off, flicker PM counts as noise of the readings too, and flicker FM is not fitted. With them on, flicker FM is fitted as well, and the model keeps the flicker FM and the flicker PM it finds as Markov states, over the averaging times at which each matters. The levels are those that the fit finds, and on a short record it can find flicker in a clock that has none: of four simulated clocks of white and random-walk FM, 2048 samples long, one got two flicker FM states.

The page forms the scale when it is shown and again when the clocks or a setting change. It needs no run.

What is shown

The upper plot gives the Allan deviation of the scale against the reference, as a solid line, and that of each clock against the scale, as dotted lines. The line above it names the algorithm, the number of samples, the sample interval and the reference, and says when flicker states are on.

With simulated clocks the reference is ideal, and the solid line is the stability of the scale itself. With measured clocks it holds the noise of the reference as well, and it shows the scale only where the reference is the more stable of the two.

Weights plots the weight of each clock against time. The table gives, for each clock:

ColumnHolds
WeightThe weight at the end of the record
MeanThe mean weight over the second half of the record
RMS errorThe rms prediction error over the second half, in seconds
White FMFor a Kalman scale, the white FM $q_1$ of the model fitted to the clock

The Jones–Tryon scale has no weights: the plot of the weights is not shown, and the two weight columns are empty.

Where the fit leaves a clock without white FM, the record does not resolve that clock. A Kalman scale then gives it nearly all of the weight and follows it, and the note under the table names it. AT1 takes no model.

With Flicker states on, the note below the table gives the number of states in the model of each clock, as 2 FM + 1 PM, or says that no clock has flicker that needs them.

The N-cornered hat

A comparison of two clocks holds the noise of both. With three or more clocks compared in pairs, the noise of each can be separated: the variance of a pair is the sum of the variances of its two clocks, if the clocks are independent, and the pairs give enough such sums to solve for every clock (Gray and Allan (1974); SP1065 §10.14). ncornered_hat gives the equations and the intervals.

The clocks of a file are read against its reference, and the page forms the difference of every pair, in which the reference cancels. The reference is then one clock more, so a file with two clocks is enough. Simulated clocks have an ideal reference, which counts as no clock: three or more are needed.

SettingEffect
MethodHat, Groslambert covariance or Groslambert, KLTS interval, described below
DeviationThe deviation that the hat separates: ADEV, MDEV, HDEV, MHDEV, PDEV, TDEV or HTDEV. The two covariance methods are defined for ADEV
Columns holdOffered for a file with three columns. Clocks: each column is a clock read against the reference. Pairs: the columns are the differences A−B, B−C and C−A of three clocks, each measured on its own
The reference counts as a clockOffered for a file with three or more clocks. Without the tick the reference is left out, and the hat has the clocks of the file alone
MethodResult
HatThe deviation of every pair, solved for each clock (ncornered_hat)
Groslambert covarianceThe average product of the second differences of two records that share a clock (groslambert; Vernotte et al. (2016)). Noise that one record of a pair has alone, such as that of its counter, stays out of the result. For three clocks read against one reference it equals the hat on ADEV, and it differs for Pairs
Groslambert, KLTS intervalThe covariance with the interval of Lantz et al. (2019) in place of the $χ^2$ interval. It is offered for three clocks, and needs a file without missing samples

The grid of averaging times and the confidence level are those of the side panel on the Stability page. Where phase columns have missing samples, the hat and the covariance leave out the terms that touch them.

The plot gives the deviation of each clock as a solid line with its confidence interval, over the deviation of each pair as measured, in thin dotted lines. The table gives each clock's deviation at the shortest averaging time and at how many averaging times it is Resolved.

The hat subtracts one measured variance from the sum of others, and the result can be negative: for a clock much more stable than the rest, for clocks whose noise is correlated, and where the samples are few, most often at long $τ$. A negative variance has no deviation. The clock is not resolved at that averaging time and has no point there. With the KLTS interval a ▽ marks the upper limit that the records set on such a clock.
