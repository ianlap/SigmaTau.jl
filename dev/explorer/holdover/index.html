Holdover

The Holdover page measures, on the record itself, how far a clock wanders once its reference is gone. A least-squares line through a training stretch of the phase gives the frequency of the clock. The clock then runs on at that frequency from the last reading of the stretch, and the error after a holdover time $τ$ is the phase that the record shows there less the phase predicted. The stretch moves along the record one sample at a time, so each holdover time has many trials. The test is that of holdover, which Holdover describes.

Settings

SettingEffect
Training stretch1/8, 1/4 or 1/2 of the record, or 2τ, a stretch twice as long as each holdover time. With the drift learned the last choice is 3τ, three times as long. The line below gives the length
Predicted byLeast-squares fit, or Kalman filter (below)
Learned from the stretchFrequency, from a line, or Frequency and drift, from a parabola
Time error limitA time error in seconds, such as 1e-6. The page then gives how long the clock stays within it. Empty for no limit
Share of trials68.3 %, 90 %, 95 % or 99 %: the share of the trials whose error the dashed curve bounds
Start from the last readingOn, the clock starts from the last reading of the stretch. Off, it starts from the value of the fit there, which averages white PM down, and on a clock with random-walk FM starts from the misfit of the line

The holdover times are the octave multiples of the sample interval, up to the length of the training stretch and no further than half of what the record has left after it. With 2τ or 3τ they go as far as leaves half the record for the trials.

The page computes when it is shown and again when the record, its preparation, its analysis range or a setting changes. It needs no run, and the estimators of the panel have no part in it. The record needs 16 samples or more. A trial is left out when missing samples take more than half of its training stretch, or the reading it starts from or is compared with.

The plot

The plot gives the time error against the holdover time, on logarithmic axes.

CurveShows
RMSThe root mean square of the error over the trials
95 % of trials, or the share chosenThe error that this share of the trials stayed within
Worst trialThe largest error of any trial
Predicted RMS (noise model)The RMS error that a power-law model of the noise predicts for the same test. The model is fitted to the modified Hadamard deviation of the record, as that of Fit on the Stability page. A model built by hand there does not enter
√6·TDEV, or √20·HTDEV with the drift learnedThe RMS error of predicting the next $τ$-average of the phase from the two before it with a line, or from the three before it with a parabola. This is what the time deviation and the Hadamard time deviation measure

With a limit set, a horizontal line marks it, and a dotted vertical line the holdover time at which the dashed curve reaches it.

The card beside the plot lists the training stretch, the holdover time within the limit, and the same time as the noise model predicts it. Below come the shares of the noise types in the predicted error variance at the longest holdover time, the ratio of the RMS to the reference curve at three holdover times, and a table of the RMS, the chosen share, the worst trial and the number of trials at each holdover time. Where the limit is passed at the first holdover time already, the card says under that time, and where it is not reached at the last, longer than that time.

Reading it

  • The trials overlap, so they are not independent. The worst trial is the worst on this record and not a bound.
  • A longer stretch averages white FM down and lets random-walk FM carry the frequency away, so the error has a minimum over the length of the stretch. Trying the three shares of the record shows which side of it the record is on.
  • A drift $D$ that the line does not learn adds $Dτ(τ + T)/2$ to every trial, with $T$ the length of the stretch. A parabola learns it, at a price that depends on the noise. In simulation, on clocks without drift, the RMS with the parabola was 1.0 to 9 times that with the line on white FM and 1.0 to 5 times on flicker FM, the more the shorter the stretch against the holdover time. On random-walk FM it was 8 to 24 % lower where the stretch was four times the holdover time or longer.
  • With the stretch 2τ (3τ), the test is the counterpart, on single readings and a least-squares fit, of the prediction that the thin reference curve measures on $τ$-averages. The two are equal at the sample interval. In simulation the RMS then runs a factor of 1.2 to 1.4 above the reference wherever FM noise dominates. Where white PM dominates the two part, because the time deviation averages the readings and the test compares single ones.
  • The predicted curve holds noise alone. A measured RMS above it at long holdover times points to drift, or to noise that the fitted model lacks.

The Kalman filter as the predictor

With Kalman filter a Kalman filter takes the place of the fit. It passes once over the record, and from every sample past the first training stretch its estimate is carried $τ$ ahead without further readings and compared with the reading there. The filter has read the whole record up to each start, and the stretch sets where the trials begin and what the filter knows of the clock: its clock model is fitted to the first training stretch alone, so the noise levels are not learned from the readings that the predictions are compared with. The model keeps the flicker FM and the flicker PM that the fit finds, as Markov states.

The training stretch is then a share of the record, Start from the last reading does not apply, and the holdover times go up to half of what the record has left after the stretch. Frequency and drift gives the model a drift state.

The gray curve is then Claimed by the filter: the standard deviation that the filter itself gives the error. The card gives the ratio of the RMS to it at the shortest and the longest holdover time, and the model: the noise of the readings R, white FM q₁ and random-walk FM q₂, and the flicker FM h₋₁ and the flicker PM h₁ where the fit finds them.

  • Where the model fits the clock the ratio is near 1. Above 1, the clock has more noise at that holdover time than the model.
  • The longest holdover times are the least certain. The RMS there rests on few independent trials, and the claim on what one stretch shows of the slow noise. In simulation, on records of 8192 samples with a quarter of the record as the stretch, the ratio at the sample interval was 0.97 to 1.04 in four records out of five, on white FM, on flicker FM, and on white FM that gives way to random-walk FM at 390 s. At 2048 s it was 0.3 to 1.3 on white FM, 0.4 to 1.3 on flicker FM, and 0.5 to 8 on the white and random-walk FM.
  • The levels are those of a fit to one stretch, which can find a noise type that the clock does not have. In the same simulation the model of 79 of the 200 white FM records held some flicker FM, and that of 48 some random-walk FM.

Several clocks

With several clocks, the settings but the last move to a bar above the plot, and each clock has one color: its RMS as a solid line and the chosen share of its trials as a dashed one. The worst trial, the prediction and the reference curve are not drawn. The card lists, for each clock, the time within the limit and the RMS at its longest holdover time.
